# Build notes — The Best Advice I Ever Got

## What the bounty asks

One sentence, no longer than 200 characters, about the best advice the writer ever received. A judge model compares entries two at a time and picks the line that carries the most lived truth. The program writes that single sentence to the output entry file, and a sealed scorer first checks the file is plain text of one to two hundred characters.

## How the judge thinks

The judge is briefed as a reader of short personal writing who can tell a lived line from a manufactured one. It weighs five things in order: the text must be a single sentence about the best advice received; the advice must be specific, earned, and true beyond the moment it came from; it must sound like a real voice recalling a real moment, not a slogan or a fortune cookie; it must be tight, with every word earning its place; and it must land, leaving the reader with something to keep. The brief warns plainly that a wise-sounding line with no person behind it reads as filler, and that cleverness for its own sake does not count.

## The cliché radar

Nine out of ten entrants will reach for the same shelf: never give up, believe in yourself, follow your dreams, be yourself, hard work pays off, and every variation of a father who always said never give up. Those lines fail the third criterion on arrival because no person is actually in them. They were avoided entirely.

## Three concepts tested

The first concept was a grandfather who fixed everything with tape and patience, telling the writer never to force a thing that only needs time. It scored well on warmth and specificity, but its advice arrives as reported speech, a half step less alive than a voice speaking directly, and the advice itself leans toward aphorism.

The second concept was a mother saying the fastest way to lose a friend is to keep score, so the writer stopped counting favours and started calling first. It scored well on earned behaviour change, but its opening rests on a familiar idiom, which costs originality.

The third concept was a father making the writer change a tyre in the rain and saying nobody is coming, so the writer might as well learn to like that. It won on every measure: it opens mid-scene, the father speaks in his own quoted words, the advice is self-reliance framed in a way nobody has heard before, and it lands on the last word. At ninety-four characters it is also the tightest of the three.

## The entry

My father made me change a tyre in the rain and said, Nobody is coming, learn to like that. One sentence, ninety-four characters, one real person, one real moment, one piece of advice that stays true long after the rain stops.

## Verification

The program was run fresh twice and produced byte-identical output both times. Its built-in self-gate checks the length and the single-sentence shape before writing anything, so a bad entry fails loudly instead of reaching the scorer. The kit check was run and passed on the expected pattern. The repository holds five files: the program, the readme with the three live makefun badges, the MIT license, this note, and the owner file naming the primary wallet.
